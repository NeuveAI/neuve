Section 3 is the port and its adapters; sections 4 to 12 are the local adapter and what every adapter shares; the Cloudflare adapter (the primary hosted design, as the owner leans) and the container adapter (the alternative) are in section 3.

**Words.** A _heavy child_ is a process Neuve starts that burns CPU for seconds or more: a Graph engine run with its `git grep`, the commit-preparation child, a stack fetch or index child, an agent's `git grep` tool call. A _permit_ is one unit of the budget (MB1); a _grant_ is the permits one heavy child or one encoder request holds. _Interactive_ work is work a person or a running review waits on; _background_ work is everything else. These are internal implementation words, not product vocabulary; the words a CLI would show are a conflict below.

## 1. What the literature says, in short

- **Capacity.** Little's law: the work in a system equals its arrival rate times the time each item spends in it ([Brooker on Little's law](https://brooker.co.za/blog/2018/06/20/littles-law.html)). At load 50 on 12 cores each runnable thread gets about a quarter of a core, so a 10-second query takes at least 40 seconds before any cache or memory contention. Amdahl's law caps a job's speed-up by its serial part. Gunther's Universal Scalability Law adds a term for the cost of keeping shared state consistent, so throughput peaks at N* = √((1 − α) ÷ β) and _falls_ beyond it; it is fitted from at least six measured points ([Gunther, USL](http://www.perfdynamics.com/Manifesto/USLscalability.html)). Size concurrency at the measured peak, never at "as much as there is work".
- **Overload.** Past a point latency grows faster than load, timeouts waste the work already done and retries add more. What matters is _useful_ throughput: work that finishes in time to be used, which the AWS article calls goodput. The remedies are bounded queues and concurrency, important work first, and measuring that useful throughput ([AWS Builders' Library, load shedding](https://builder.aws.com/content/3Eun1EEyX6p2e3VYNyRLSJzLuMV/using-load-shedding-to-avoid-overload)). Google measures capacity in CPU rather than requests and sheds the least critical work first ([Google SRE, handling overload](https://sre.google/sre-book/handling-overload/)). Bulkheads keep separate pools so one kind of work cannot exhaust what another needs ([Azure bulkhead pattern](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/architecture/patterns/bulkhead)).
- **Adaptive limits.** Netflix's concurrency-limits library borrows from TCP's congestion control. Its Vegas algorithm reads a rise in latency above the fastest seen as the sign that work has started to queue, and lowers the limit; a gradient variant compares short and long latency averages. A partitioned limiter reserves shares of one limit for classes of work, for example 90% live and 10% batch ([Netflix concurrency-limits](https://github.com/Netflix/concurrency-limits); [Performance Under Load](https://netflixtechblog.medium.com/performance-under-load-3e6fa9a60581)).
- **BullMQ.** A worker's own concurrency sets how many jobs it runs at once; a queue's global concurrency caps the total across every worker ([worker concurrency](https://docs.bullmq.io/guide/workers/concurrency), [global concurrency](https://docs.bullmq.io/guide/queues/global-concurrency)). A rate limiter caps jobs per time window across workers, and a job that meets an external limit (an HTTP 429) can put the whole queue on hold ([rate limiting](https://docs.bullmq.io/guide/rate-limiting)). Priorities run lowest number first, first-in-first-out within one ([priorities](https://docs.bullmq.io/guide/jobs/prioritized)). A job whose worker stops signalling goes back to waiting, up to a stall limit ([stalled jobs](https://docs.bullmq.io/guide/jobs/stalled)). Pro's groups take jobs round-robin across groups, so one busy group cannot block the rest ([groups](https://docs.bullmq.io/bullmq-pro/groups)).
- **Cross-process budgets.** GNU make's jobserver shares a fixed number of job tokens across recursive makes and their children through a pipe, and a client that dies holding tokens loses them ([GNU make, job slots](https://www.gnu.org/software/make/manual/html_node/Job-Slots.html); the lost-token behaviour from the jobserver documentation, not re-read here). A connection-held grant (section 4) avoids that loss, because the kernel closes a dead process's connections.
- **Operating system.** On Apple silicon, threads at background quality of service run only on the efficiency cores and are never promoted; utility and higher prefer the performance cores; `taskpolicy -b` demotes a process and its children ([Oakley, Quality of Service](https://eclecticlight.co/2025/05/09/what-is-quality-of-service-and-how-does-it-matter/); his remark that renicing showed no effect on Apple silicon is from a comment reply). On Linux, cgroups v2 give `cpu.weight`, `cpu.max`, `cpu.idle` and a CPU pressure file ([kernel cgroup v2](https://docs.kernel.org/admin-guide/cgroup-v2.html)). Linux load averages also count tasks blocked on disk, so load is a weak signal on its own there ([Gregg, Linux load averages](https://www.brendangregg.com/blog/2017-08-08/linux-load-averages.html)).

## 2. The unit and the size

**Unit.** One permit is one thread that may be runnable at once: roughly one core's worth of CPU. A grant holds as many permits as the thread cap Neuve gives the child, and Neuve always sets that cap: `git -c grep.threads=k`, `GOMAXPROCS=k`, the encoder's PyTorch threads, `pack.threads` and `index.threads`. A child whose threads cannot be capped is weighted by its measured average parallelism (CPU time ÷ wall time, rounded up). A Graph run counts the engine and its `git` together, at their measured parallelism. Counting processes would undercount an 8-thread `git`; counting the threads the OS reports would overcount threads that sleep.

**Size.** Let P be the performance cores (all cores where there is no split, as on Linux or Intel) and N all logical cores. The default budget is B = max(1, min(P, N − 2)): 8 here, 4 on a base M1 (4 + 4), 2 on a 4-core Linux machine, 1 on a 2-core one. Inside a container or a Linux cgroup, N and P come from the CPU quota (`cpu.max`), not the host's cores. The owner can set B in user-level settings. Measurement (section 10) may lower the default; it never raises it above P.

## 3. One admission port, an adapter per platform (10.92)

**The port.** Callers see one small interface, the way 8.12's `JobQueue` service already hides the queue and 11.12's storage interface hides where proof lives:

- `admit({ owner, class, permits, kind })` returns a scoped grant: the work runs inside it and the grant ends when the work ends, however it ends.
- `capacity()` reports the budget, the reserve and the background cap (section 6) as this platform sizes them.
- `observe()` reports grants held and waited for, by class and owner, for telemetry and the CLI.

Today's `HeavyWork.start` becomes the local adapter's implementation of `admit`. Job kinds keep their policies (slot, priority, retries, deadline, stall limit); an adapter maps each slot onto its platform's queue.

**What carries over unchanged to every platform:** job kinds as queue messages with their policies; the permit as the unit (one thread, or one vCPU's worth); one admission point with the interactive reserve, the background cap and fairness by owner; a grant per heavy unit of work, taken in one request (section 5); bounded concurrency inside a job; reducing work before parallelising; the bench, the choice rule and the stopping rule (sections 10 and 14), re-run on each platform's shape.

**What is local only:** process groups and forced kills of children; `taskpolicy`, macOS priority classes, launchd's `ProcessType` and the performance and efficiency core split; the user-level budget socket under `/tmp/neuve-<uid>` and its host claim file (round 0's user-level ledger file is dropped); the load-average brake (section 11). Linux nice is the Unix-friendly default for any adapter that starts processes; macOS specifics live in the local adapter only.

**The three adapters.** The owner leans towards Cloudflare for a hosted Neuve, so the Cloudflare adapter is the primary hosted design and the container adapter is the alternative that stays viable. Where the two differ, the local design picks the shape that maps onto Queues and Durable Objects.

| | Local (today) | Cloudflare: Workers, Queues, Durable Objects, Workflows (primary hosted) | Unix container: Cloud Run, GKE or similar, from a Docker image (alternative) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Unit of work | A heavy child process, or an encoder request | A Workflow step, or one queue consumer invocation; there are no child processes. Heavy native work (Graph, the encoder) runs in a Cloudflare Container (at most 4 vCPU an instance), admitted as one unit per request | A heavy child process inside an instance |
| Budget size | B from the cores (section 2) | Each unit is bounded by the platform's per-invocation CPU (30 s by default, up to 5 minutes); the budget is a count of units in flight, per class, and for Containers the instances allowed (`max_instances`) | Per instance: the CPU quota (`cpu.max`; Cloud Run allows up to 8 vCPU an instance, GKE's CPU limit is a hard throttle). Across instances: the queue's dispatch limit times the instance count |
| Coordination point | The worker hosting the budget socket (section 4) | A Durable Object per budget, running the scheduler of section 6: each object is single-threaded, so it serializes every decision. An expired lease frees the grant; after a deploy, holders reconnect and reconcile their rows | Inside an instance, the in-memory semaphore of its one worker process. | Measuring | The bench on this machine | The bench as steps, measuring CPU time per step against the limit | The bench in one instance, then with several |

**Keeping the local design portable to Cloudflare.** Three choices in the local adapter are made so the move maps cleanly:

- _The same port and the same scheduler._ A client asks for permits, is told when they are granted, and releases them; the scheduler's rules are identical. Only how a dead holder is noticed differs: locally the connection's close, on Cloudflare a lease in the Durable Object's storage, because its WebSockets drop on every deploy and its memory resets on hibernation. Callers do not change.
- _A grant is per unit of work, not per process_ (section 5), so the long-lived encoder's grant becomes one per request, which is what a request to a container is on Cloudflare.
- _Slots are per class, not per process._ The worker's two slots (10.31) are kept as the interactive and background classes the port knows, so on Cloudflare they become two queues with their own consumer concurrency, not a loop inside a process.

What would make the move harder, and stays behind the port or is flagged:

- _Long-lived processes._ The worker, the budget host and the encoder all live across many jobs. On Cloudflare there is no such process: the Durable Object holds the state and Workers run per invocation. Nothing outside the local adapter may rely on the host being one process, or on in-memory state surviving between jobs.
- _Shared files._ The per-repository SQLite store and queue (8.3, 8.12), the budget socket under `/tmp` and its claim file, Graph's private clones and snapshot cache, and the run folder's working copies (10.14) all assume a shared disk. A Durable Object per repository, with its own SQLite storage (10 GB each on the paid plan), is the natural home of a repository's queue state and outbox, and its single thread gives the single writer that 8.2 now gets from a socket claim. Graph's clones and caches are the hardest part to move: a Container's disk is ephemeral, so they are lost whenever it sleeps, and would have to be rebuilt or kept in object storage. That is an open point.
- _Process groups and forced kills._ Cancelling work is local only. Callers end work by ending the scoped grant (`admit`), never by signalling processes, so on Cloudflare the same cancellation stops a Workflow step or a Container request.
- _The load-average brake_ reads the host's load; on Cloudflare the platform schedules CPU, and the brake does nothing.

Sources: [Cloud Run concurrency](https://docs.cloud.google.com/run/docs/about-concurrency), [Cloud Run CPU](https://docs.cloud.google.com/run/docs/configuring/services/cpu), [Kubernetes CPU requests and limits](https://kubernetes.io/docs/concepts/configuration/manage-resources-containers/), [Cloud Tasks queue settings](https://docs.cloud.google.com/tasks/docs/configuring-queues), [Pub/Sub subscription properties](https://docs.cloud.google.com/pubsub/docs/subscription-properties), [Workers limits](https://developers.cloudflare.com/workers/platform/limits/), [Queues limits](https://developers.cloudflare.com/queues/platform/limits/), [Queues consumer concurrency](https://developers.cloudflare.com/queues/configuration/consumer-concurrency/), [Durable Objects limits](https://developers.cloudflare.com/durable-objects/platform/limits/), [Durable Objects WebSockets](https://developers.cloudflare.com/durable-objects/best-practices/websockets/), [Workflows limits](https://developers.cloudflare.com/workflows/reference/limits/), [Containers](https://developers.cloudflare.com/containers/).

**Heavy work on Workers.** Graph is a Go program that runs `git`, and the encoder is a Python process; neither runs inside a Worker, whose isolate has 128 MB and no child processes. On Cloudflare they would run in its Containers, which a Worker starts and reaches only through a Durable Object. A Container has at most 4 vCPU and an ephemeral disk, so its budget is at most 4 and anything cached on disk (Graph's clones and snapshots) is gone after it sleeps. The container adapter's rules apply inside it, and the Cloudflare adapter admits the requests to it. What runs as Worker steps is the JavaScript-only work: model calls, Records writes, composing the review.

**Does the job model map onto each queue?** 8.12's queue has: the job written in the same transaction as its event; de-duplication by key; retries with backoff; a final failed state with its cause; recovery of jobs a dead worker left active, up to a stall limit; a deadline per attempt; and (10.31) slots and priorities.

| --- | --- | --- | --- |

So the model maps, with three changes the hosted adapters must make: de-duplication moves into Neuve's own store, enqueueing goes through an outbox, and abandoned work is recovered by a timed lease, which conflicts with 8.2 (section 16). Cloudflare maps more directly than the container path: a Durable Object per repository gives the single writer and the transactional outbox in one place, where the container path needs a shared database for both.

## 4. The local adapter: one host for the machine

**Options.**

| | A. Each process limits itself (today) | B. A user-level permit ledger file (round 0's recommendation) | C. The job queue's slots only; outside processes ask their repository's worker | D. As C, with one worker hosting the budget for the machine |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Covers other repositories | No | Yes | No: two repositories' workers make 2B | Yes |
| Covers benchmark and check runs | No | Yes | Yes, through the worker's socket | Yes, through the budget socket |
| Clears a dead holder | — | By process liveness, which sandboxed sessions cannot check (`ps`, `kill -0`), and which breaks across devcontainers' separate process-ID spaces | The kernel closes a dead holder's connection | The same |
| Wakes a waiter | — | By polling, with no order between waiters | A push over the connection, in order | The same |
| Admission points | One, but beside the queue rather than through it, against 10.91's wording | Two: the queue and a file | One: the worker's slots | One: the worker's slots draw from the same budget other processes ask |

**Recommendation: D (flipped from B in round 1).** The budget is the scheduler of section 6, in memory in one worker. That worker's slots draw their jobs' grants from it directly, so the queue stays the admission point for product work, as 10.91 says. Other workers and processes that are not jobs (the benchmark's lead ranking, a harness check) ask it over a Unix socket, `budget.sock`, in the private directory the worker socket's fallback already uses (`/tmp/neuve-<uid>`, `0700`; the socket `0600`).

- **Only workers host.** A CLI or benchmark process never hosts, so there are no short-lived hosts. A process that needs permits and finds no host starts its repository's worker with the existing starter and connects to it; outside any Neuve repository it runs at the floor (below).
- **Choosing the host, race-free.** The same claim the worker already makes for its repository (8.2), on a small user-level SQLite file beside 8.6's repository list (`budget.sqlite`, `0600`). In one `BEGIN IMMEDIATE` transaction a starting worker connects to `budget.sock`: a connection accepted means a host runs, and this worker becomes a client. A socket that refuses is removed only when the recorded host (its process ID and the kernel's start time for it) is shown gone; a refusal alone is also what a loaded host gives when its accept queue is full, so a busy host is never taken for dead. Then it binds and records itself. SQLite's write lock serializes starters, so two can never both bind.
- **A grant follows the child.** A grant is held by a connection, and the host frees it when the connection closes. The client opens one connection per grant and passes its file descriptor to the heavy child as an extra standard-stream descriptor, so the connection closes only when both the parent and the child have closed it: a parent that crashes while its child runs keeps the grant until the child ends. This works in Bun 1.4.0, as the reviewer probed: the descriptor of a `node:net` client connection, read from its internal `_handle.fd`, is passed at standard-stream index 3, and the host saw the close only when the child exited. It relies on an undocumented internal, so it is pinned by a test and checked on each Bun upgrade (Bun's types document a plain descriptor number at index 3 and above). Graph's engine is a Go program, and Go's `os/exec` is believed to leave inherited descriptors open, so its `git` children probably hold the grant too, which helps; that is unverified.
- **The host dies.** Its connections close, so every client sees the loss. Running children keep running; a new host is elected as above; each client reconnects and re-declares the grants of its children still running, and the new host makes no new grant for 5 seconds while they do. A child whose parent also died is not re-declared, so the new host may over-admit by that child until it ends. That is the one gap. Only Graph's grep scan has a deadline of its own (5 minutes); Graph preparation, commit preparation and the stack children rely on their parent to stop them, so such an orphan runs until it finishes or is ended by hand (below).
- **The host exits.** A worker that hosts exits idle only when it also holds no open grants, claims or waiters (section 5's shared work; a change to 6.9's idle exit, section 16). When it does exit, nothing is in flight; the next worker or starter claims the host.
- **Versions.** A client's first message names the protocol version. A host that speaks another version answers so, and the client runs at the floor and says why, which covers an installed `neuve` and a workspace copy running side by side (6.5). There are never two hosts.
- **Hung holders and orphans.** A grant ends with its child. A child is stopped at its job's deadline by its parent; only Graph's grep scan also stops itself. An orphan whose parent died therefore keeps its grant until it ends, which is correct accounting (it still burns CPU) but can hold permits for long. The CLI can end a grant by hand and kill the holder's process group. Giving Neuve's own Bun children a deadline of their own (the job's, passed at start) closes the gap for them; Graph preparation would need an engine-side deadline. Both are later.

**The floor, when the host cannot be reached.** A sandboxed agent session may be unable to connect to `/tmp/neuve-<uid>`, and `taskpolicy -b` itself fails there, so wrapping the child would stop it from starting at all. Such a process runs its heavy work with no class wrapper, at nice 19 (a process may always lower its own priority), one child at a time, with thread caps of 1, and its run's receipt says so. This is fail-open: failing closed would stop reviews and checks in sandboxed sessions altogether. Honestly, the total cannot be bounded by Neuve: each sandboxed session adds at most one thread at nice 19, so the total is one thread per such session, and Neuve cannot count them. The owner ruled that sandboxed sessions are allowed to reach the budget socket in their sandbox settings (10.93), so the floor remains only for a session not set up that way. The remedy outside Neuve is to let the sandbox reach the socket: Claude Code's sandbox settings can allow a Unix socket path, which brings those sessions under the budget (unverified for this setup).

**Devcontainers.** A container has its own process-ID space and its own `/tmp`, so it cannot reach the host's budget, but on a Mac it runs on the same cores (inside Docker's virtual machine). Its budget must come from a CPU quota set for the container (`--cpus`), chosen so the host's budget and the containers' quotas together stay within the machine; without a quota, each container would size itself from all the virtual machine's cores and stack on the host's budget. Mounting the socket directory into the container would share one budget instead; that is left open.

**How wide the change is.** `HeavyWork.start` covers commit preparation and the stack handlers today, inside a worker only. Under D it takes `{ permits, class }`, reaches the budget from any process, and must be called at every heavy spawn, and only there:

1. graph-client's one `command` function, which starts every Graph preparation and query;
2. the encoder's request path in semantic-client, per request. The encoder fixes 4 PyTorch threads when it starts (`semantic-client/src/worker.py`, line 91), so a grant capped or cut below 4 would under-count it. Recommended: each request carries its thread count in the protocol, and the encoder calls `torch.set_num_threads` with the grant before encoding. Fallback, if that proves unsafe or slow: the encoder takes a fixed grant of 4 that is exempt from cutting and capping, and waits until 4 are free;
3. the harness's `git.ts`, which runs agents' `git grep`. It runs light calls too (`update-ref`, `config`, small reads), so it gains a `heavy` flag: only calls marked heavy, such as `git grep` over a tree, take a grant (1 permit per grep thread), and light calls never queue behind a 4-permit request;
4. the commit-preparation child starter;
5. and 6. the `stack.fetch` and `stack.index` child starters, each taking one grant for the child's whole thread cap;
6. the benchmark's own `git clone` and `update-ref` spawns in `retrieval-bench/graph.ts` (lines 158–220), with the same `heavy` flag, so only the clone takes a grant. Its Graph inspections and preparations go through graph-client and are covered by site 1, never here, so they are never granted twice;
7. the benchmark's own encoder process in `retrieval-bench/encoder.ts`, per request;
8. the benchmark's git reads in `retrieval-bench/git.ts`, with the `heavy` flag.

That is nine call sites in five packages, plus the scheduler, the host and the client. The stack ranking's `git grep` (`stack/rank.ts`) runs only inside the `stack.fetch` child, so that child's grant covers it.

**The nesting rule.** A grant is taken only at the spawn, after every in-process lock the caller needs (Graph's one-query slot and its per-clone lock), and nothing is taken while a grant is held. A tool call takes no grant of its own: the `git` or Graph process it starts takes it at the spawn. A child that starts processes of its own stays within its one grant. Locks are always taken before grants, never after, so a grant holder never waits for a lock and a lock holder waits only for the budget, which never waits for anything. Light spawns (launchd, transcripts, proof reads, `git cat-file` reads of a few blobs) stay outside.

## 5. What a grant covers, and why it cannot deadlock

**Options.** Per job: a job takes its whole grant when its slot takes it, and splits it among its children. Per child: each heavy child, or each encoder request, takes its own grant just before it starts and returns it when it ends.

**Recommendation: per child.** A review run spends most of its time waiting on the model, so a job-long grant would hold permits that do nothing; the long-lived encoder would hold its 4 permits while idle. Per child, the encoder takes 4 permits per request and none between requests.

**Deadlock.** A cycle needs a holder that waits for more. The rule that prevents it: _a holder never waits for permits_. Each child takes everything it needs in one request at its spawn (a Graph run with k concurrent scans asks for all of them at once), nothing is taken while a grant is held, and in-process locks are taken before the grant, never after (the nesting rule, section 4). A child that starts its own `git` processes, such as the commit-preparation child, stays within its one grant and never asks again. The scheduler (section 6) grants a request only whole, so no waiter ever holds part of what it asked for. Running children only finish and release, so every waiter is eventually served. The per-job model avoids deadlock by the same rule, one up-front take and no further take while holding, but wastes the permits. The other deadlock that the independent review of slice H2 (the worker's two slots and the heavy-work gate) found, a review waiting on a computation that a background job holds while that job waits for the review, is avoided by the shared-work rule below: _work that holds a grant (a heavy child and the fiber waiting on it) never waits for a key another process holds_. A computation's holder may wait for grants (an E0 query's encoder request inside a stage), but whoever already holds a grant never waits on a key, so no cycle closes through a key.

### Shared work: computed once, across jobs and processes

The owner's note on accepting this design (21 October 2026): the queue and its events must let a job use a part another job is producing, never compute it twice, because avoiding duplicated work is already a base principle (5.5, 10.75).

**Today.** Every stage is a computation keyed by its inputs and its operation's version, and a stored result is reused. Inside one process, concurrent requests for one key already share one attempt: `computations.ts` keeps a map of keys in flight, each with a `Deferred` that the waiters await before reading the stored result (lines 225–227 and 431–466). Across processes nothing is shared. `start` refuses a key that is already stored, but not one whose attempt is still running (lines 255–286), so two processes on one store can compute the same key at once. The answer stays correct (the first `succeed` binds the result and the second reads it), but the work is done twice. Jobs are de-duplicated by job identity (`commit.prepare:<oid>`, `review.run:<runKey>:<generation>`, `jobs.ts` lines 17 and 39), never by computation key, and the worker's single claim only stops two workers on one store. The main case is inside one worker: the commit-preparation child is its own process, opens the store itself (`worker-cli.ts` lines 560–575) and computes hunks, the search index and hunk leads (`commit-prepare.ts` lines 111–165), the same keys a review of that commit needs, while the review slot runs beside it. Others: an orphaned commit-preparation child (section 4 keeps it alive) beside a new worker, and the harness check's `seed`, `check-leads` and `served` commands pointed at a live repository.

**What the literature does.** Request coalescing, or single flight (Go's `singleflight`; Effect's `Cache`, whose concurrent `get`s for one missing key share one lookup): one execution per key, and every concurrent caller awaits its result. Results keyed by their inputs (the action caches of Bazel and Nix's input-addressed store): equal inputs reuse and different inputs never collide. BullMQ: a duplicate add is ignored and reported as de-duplicated, and a caller waits for the existing job through its job events (`QueueEvents`); in its flows a parent waits in a waiting-children state, woken by its children's completion rather than polling. Priority inheritance (from real-time scheduling): a high-priority waiter lends its priority to the low-priority work it waits on.

**Recommendation.**

- **One claim per key, held like a grant.** The budget host also keeps the keys in flight, keyed by the resolved store (its git common directory, `location.ts` lines 43–63) and the computation key, so a waiter always reads the result from the store the holder writes. Before an attempt starts, its process claims the key over the budget socket. The claim is a connection, like a grant, so it ends when the claiming process ends, with no timed lock (8.2). A second claimant for a held key waits on that connection, and the host pushes when the holder's claim ends. The in-process map stays in front of the claim, so one process asks the host once per key.
- **The result travels through the store, the wake-up through the push.** The holder writes the result and its completion in one transaction, as today, and releases its claim only after that transaction commits. The push only wakes the waiters, which then read the stored result; a waiter never takes the result from the push.
- **Work that holds a grant never waits on a key.** The rule is scoped to the work holding the grant, the heavy child and the fiber waiting on it, not to the whole process: the worker keeps its own copy of its child's grant connection, and its review slot may still wait on the child's keys. Waiting for a key happens before any grant is taken, like an in-process lock (the nesting rule, section 4). A claim made from inside a grant carries an "inside a grant" flag; if the key is held, it is told "held" and does not wait: the commit-preparation child, which computes inside its one background grant, skips that item and leaves it to the review that holds it, which is safe because commit preparation is only precomputation. A key's holder may wait for grants (for example an E0 query's encoder request inside a stage), so this rule is what closes the cycle a review and the commit-preparation child could otherwise form on a small machine, where background's cap plus a review's request is more than the budget.
- **No cycle through nested keys.** Computations do nest (an E0 query inside an agent stage), but nested computations follow the pipeline's stage order, which has no cycles, so no key's holder ever waits, directly or through others, on a key that waits on it.
- **Failure.** If the holder's attempt fails, the waiters get its recorded cause (today's `SharedAttemptFailed`) and their own job's retry policy decides; the retrying job then claims the key and becomes the holder. If the holder's process dies, its claim's connection closes and the host offers the claim to the first waiter, which marks the dead holder's running attempt for that key abandoned in its own store before it starts (the host may be another repository's worker and never writes into this store). The worker's start-up sweep (`recoverAbandoned`, `worker.ts` line 956), which today abandons every running attempt in the store, skips attempts that still have a live claim, and a worker that has just become the host runs it only after the 5-second re-declaration window closes, so it never abandons a live orphan's or a harness check's attempt.
- **Losing the host.** A waiter that loses the host re-reads the store first, then claims again at the new host; losing the host is never reported as the holder failing. Holders re-declare their claims together with their grants, in the same 5-second window (section 4), and the host's idle exit counts claims and waiters as well as grants. If a holder is not re-declared (its parent died too), a new claimant may compute beside it; the answer stays correct, as today, and only that work is duplicated.
- **Priority inheritance, as far as it reaches.** When a review waits on a key that background work holds, the host moves the holder's grant requests that are still waiting, and any it makes later, to the interactive class. A running child keeps its class, because a background class cannot be lifted from outside (starting facts). The commit-preparation child asks for no grant after its first, so a review waiting on it waits at the efficiency cores' speed; that case is bounded by the child's deadline, and when it matters the review can claim the next keys itself, since the child skips held keys.
- **When the host cannot be reached** (the floor), a process falls back to today's behaviour, computing the key itself, and its receipt says the work may have been duplicated.
- **Hosted.** On Cloudflare the repository's Durable Object already serializes that repository's queue and records, so it holds the keys in flight in its storage; a claim there is a leased row like a grant (8.2 scoped to local, section 16), and waiting review runs are woken by an event (a Workflow's wait for an event, to be confirmed when hosted work starts). Claims live in the repository's object and grants in the budget's object (section 3), so priority inheritance is a message from the first to the second. On the container path a claim is a leased row in the shared database, expiring like an acknowledgement deadline, so a dead holder's row cannot strand its waiters.

**Not proposed.** Partial results: a waiter waits for the whole computation and never reads it mid-flight. Sharing across stores: keys are per store, as today, except where a store is already user-level (stack sources share by tree id, 11.15).

## 6. Classes, shares and fairness

**Classes.** Interactive grants serve a review run's heavy children (its Graph queries, its agents' `git grep`, its encoder queries). Background grants serve commit preparation, stack fetch and index, and the benchmark's batch work.

**Shares.** Interactive may use the whole budget. A reserve R stays free for interactive: R = 0 when B = 1, otherwise max(1, ⌊B ÷ 4⌋). Background may hold at most max(1, min(B − R, E)), where E is the efficiency cores on a machine that has them, because background children run there (section 8) and more background permits than efficiency cores only queue on those cores.

| Machine | B | R | Background at most |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 2-core Linux | 1 | 0 | 1 |
| 4-core Linux | 2 | 1 | 1 |
| Base M1 (4 performance, 4 efficiency) | 4 | 1 | 3 |
| This Mac (8 performance, 4 efficiency) | 8 | 2 | 4 |

**A background minimum.** Rule 2 below serves interactive first, so a steady stream of interactive requests could keep background waiting for ever. To prevent that, whenever background has waiters, interactive may hold at most B − 1 permits, leaving one for background. A background request larger than what is free for it would still never fit, so when background's turn comes under saturation, its request at the front is cut to what is free for it (at least 1), granted at once, and the caller sets its thread caps to the grant, as rule 1 already allows for caps. A cut grant never grows later, so nothing holds part of a request while waiting for the rest. On a machine with B = 1 the two classes take turns instead when both wait. The alternative, aging (a waiting background request gains priority over time), also prevents starvation but is harder to reason about; the minimum is recommended. The cost is one permit less for a review while background work waits. When background holds its 4 efficiency-core permits here, interactive has 4 of the 8, leaving performance cores free for the person; whether interactive should borrow beyond that is measured, not assumed.

**The scheduler.** Neither of Effect's semaphores fits. The plain `Semaphore` lets small requests overtake a large one indefinitely; the `PartitionedSemaphore` grants permits partially and hands released permits out round-robin, which deadlocks: with 8 permits and two waiters of 6 in two partitions, each ends up holding some and waiting for the rest (reproduced by the reviewer). Neither knows classes or caps. So the budget is a small scheduler of Neuve's own, about a hundred lines on Effect's `Ref` (the state: permits free, permits held by class, the waiters) and `Deferred` (one per waiter, completed when it is granted). Its rules:

1. **Whole grants only.** A request is granted all its permits at once or none; the one exception is the background request cut to fit under the minimum (above), which is still granted at once and never grows. A request above its cap is cut to the cap when it is made, and the caller sets its thread caps to what it got, so every request can eventually fit. Background requests are capped at background's cap; any single interactive request is capped at ⌈B ÷ 2⌉ (4 here).
2. **Classes in order.** Interactive waiters are served before background ones. While an interactive request waits, no background grant is made even if one would fit, except the background minimum above.
3. **Caps.** A background grant must keep background's holdings within its cap (above) and, once it is built, the brake (section 11).
4. **First-in-first-out within a class, round-robin across owners, blocking at the front.** Each owner (a repository's worker, or an outside session) has its own queue per class; the scheduler takes owners in turn, and within an owner the oldest request first. If the request whose turn it is does not fit, the scheduler waits for releases rather than serving a smaller one or skipping to the next owner. Skipping would let a large request starve behind a stream of small ones; blocking does not. The cost is head-of-line blocking: permits may sit idle while enough of them free up for the request at the front. Capping a single request at ⌈B ÷ 2⌉ (rule 1) bounds that: at most ⌈B ÷ 2⌉ permits wait idle, the ⌈B ÷ 2⌉ − 1 that fall short of the request at the front, plus the one kept for background while background waits but cannot take it (at its cap). That is 4 here.
5. **A holder never waits.** Admission is the only place a process waits, and nothing it holds is counted as waiting. An interrupted waiter (a cancelled job, the run's cut-off) is removed from its queue.

The scheduler runs again after every release and every new request. It is the same logic in every adapter; on Cloudflare its state lives in the Durable Object's storage (section 3).

**No preemption.** A waiting interactive request is served as soon as enough running children end; because no new background grant is made meanwhile beyond the background minimum, the wait is bounded by the children already running. Their jobs' deadlines bound them while their parents live; an orphan is bounded only as section 4 says.

**The gate.** This replaces the heavy-work gate's all-or-nothing rule with a share. Background work keeps moving during a review, on the efficiency cores, and the review keeps its reserve. If measurement shows reviews still slow down, the background share during a review drops toward zero, which is today's gate as one end of the same setting.

**Fairness across repositories and sessions** comes from rule 4, as BullMQ Pro's groups take jobs round-robin across groups: one repository's commit burst cannot hold back another's review, and the benchmark cannot hold back either.

## 7. Inside a job and a review

A job's children are started with bounded concurrency (`Effect.forEach(items, f, { concurrency: k })`), never `"unbounded"` around a spawn; fibers that only wait on the network may stay unbounded, because the grant at the spawn is the bound. Every child runs in its own process group through Effect's `ChildProcess` or graph-client's equivalent, with a kill signal and a forced kill after a grace, so cancelling a job stops everything it started and returns its grants.

**Tool calls.** Today an agent's tool calls run inside its model-call permit, all at once. A tool waiting for CPU permits would then hold a model-call slot doing nothing. The recommendation: take the model-call permit inside the HTTP client transform that `agent.ts` already installs (`AnthropicConfig.withClientTransform`), so it covers exactly one request to the provider. Tool calls then run after the request returns, outside the permit, and so do the waits between retries, because each retry is a new request. The permit's place depends on the route, and each route's model adapter owns it. On the Anthropic route the request is not streamed, so the client transform covers it. The OpenAI route has its own model adapter and streams its answer (server-sent events, `openai-route.ts`, lines 377 and 770); a transform would release the permit when the headers arrive, while the model is still generating. There the permit wraps the request and the reading of the stream to its end, inside the route's adapter. `generateText` is given a `concurrency` of 2, so one response cannot start every tool at once, and a tool takes no grant of its own: the process it starts takes an interactive grant at its spawn (the nesting rule, section 4).

The alternative, turning off Effect's tool resolution (`disableToolCallResolution`) and running the tools in the agent loop, is not recommended. `Chat` saves the history after each `generateText`, so a crash between saving the model's tool calls and saving their results would leave calls with no results, and resuming (10.14) would need a repair rule: re-run those calls, or record them as failed, before the next turn. The transform avoids that.

## 8. Scheduling class and nice (local adapter)

- **macOS.** Background children start under `taskpolicy -b`, so every `git` they start inherits it: they run on the efficiency cores with throttled disk access. Interactive children run at the default class and **nice 0**: they are what a review waits on, and the budget, not a lower priority, bounds them. Nice 10 would only let other sessions' builds overtake the review.
- **Linux.** Background children run at nice 19 and, where a user systemd manager exists, in a scope with a low `cpu.weight`. Interactive children run at nice 0.
- **Workers start at the default class.** Because `taskpolicy -B` does not lift a background class a process inherited, a worker cannot fix this itself. Every starter must start workers at the default class: the hooks (which start from the user's or harness's shell), and the launchd sweep, whose agent moves from `ProcessType Background` to `Standard`. The sweep is a short scan and lowers its own nice.
- The bench (section 10) checks whether nice changes anything on Apple silicon. If it does not, nice stays for Linux and the class does the work on macOS.

## 9. Deadlines and waits

A permit wait does not count against a Graph query's own deadline: the deadline starts when the grant is made. The wait has its own cap (the same 5 minutes); past it the query returns "Graph busy", a disclosed gap.

A review run's permit waits **do** count against its lifetime. 10.15 says a review must never take more than 10 minutes and 10.26 makes that the run's whole lifetime, so the round-1 allowance of 2 minutes outside it is dropped. The interactive reserve and class order keep the waits short; telemetry records them, so a run that loses time to waiting shows it. At the 9:45 cut-off (10.42), when the run stops starting new work, any request still waiting is withdrawn, as rule 5 of the scheduler allows. A background job's waits stay out of its deadline, as the gate's are today.

## 10. Measuring and tuning

A concurrency bench, run by hand like the benchmark, never in `bun run check`. Only its Graph part, in minimal form, is in the first slice (section 14); the rest comes later:

1. **Workloads, pinned:** Graph searches over the stalled bases from 10.90 and a sample of dev hunks; commit preparation over a pinned burst of about 30 commits; a stack fetch and index from a local mirror.
2. **Background load, realistic:** a generator running what this machine carries at the same time: another session's `bun run check` loop and two encoders, at normal priority. Each point runs idle and then under that load.
3. **Sweep:** concurrency 1, 2, 4, 6, 8 and 12 (past B on purpose, to see the fall), three repeats each.
4. **Recorded:** throughput (searches or commits per minute), median and 95th-percentile task time, an interactive probe (a small `neuve jobs` and a `git grep` over a small tree, once a second, at normal priority), the 1-minute load, the run queue (on Linux the CPU pressure file), and CPU time per child to check each weight.
5. **Fit:** the Universal Scalability Law to throughput, giving its peak.

**Choosing.** The smallest concurrency whose throughput is within 10% of the fitted peak and whose probe stays within 1.5 times its idle value at the 95th percentile. The chosen sizes, the machine and the date are recorded beside the rulings. Telemetry (10.16) keeps the same measures from real runs: grants held and waited for, by class and owner, and wait times.

## 11. A brake for load Neuve does not control (local adapter)

Much of the load behind 10.91 comes from outside Neuve, such as agent sessions running checks. The budget cannot govern that, so the background share has a brake. It reads `os.loadavg()` when a grant is asked for, so no process has to keep running to watch (6.9), and on Linux the CPU pressure file where it exists.

- When the 1-minute load exceeds the core count, the background cap halves, at most once a minute, never below 1.
- It grows back one permit every 30 seconds, only while the load stays below 0.7 times the core count.

The gap between the two thresholds and the once-a-minute limit keep it from swinging: the 1-minute load lags, and halving on every reading would keep cutting before the last cut shows. Interactive grants are never braked. A full adaptive limiter like Netflix's is not proposed until the bench shows the fixed budget plus this brake mis-sizes.

## 12. The model calls

The 8 model calls in flight (10.27) wait on the network and the provider, not on this machine's CPU, so they stay out of the CPU budget. Their limit is the provider's. Three changes are proposed: the permit covers only the provider request, so tool calls and the waits between retries no longer hold it (section 7); a rate-limit response (HTTP 429) puts every run on that route on hold for the time the provider asks, as BullMQ's manual rate limit does; and a machine-wide cap per route, across runs, only if telemetry shows 429s when runs overlap.

## 13. First applications

**Graph searches (10.91's named first case).** Reduce the work first, then parallelise, with byte-identical results, because the benchmark's pinned answers depend on them (10.90).

1. _Cache by tree and pattern (later)._ A tree never changes, so a pattern's matches at a tree never change, and hunks at the same base share most patterns. A cache keyed by tree and pattern would remove repeat scans across hunks; it is not part of what the Graph lane built.
2. _Reduce the work (built)._ The Graph lane measured the candidate-file pre-pass and dropped it: it listed 91–97% of files and cost about half the scans. Instead each alias group is split into its alternatives, with identical matches, which cut that pattern's scan from 18.1 to 3.4 s.
3. _Bounded concurrent scans (built)._ The per-pattern scans run in a bounded pool, one `git grep` thread per process. The engine's own budget defaults to half the performance cores, 4 here, so 4 scans at a time of 1 thread each. Measured over 12 settings of scans and threads on 4 queries, idle and loaded: single-threaded processes were as fast or faster than multi-threaded ones and used 10–35% less CPU, and gains flatten after about 6. The worst query's grep fell from 29.9 to 7.9 s and the whole query from 49 to 28 s, with identical output. `ENTIRE_GRAPH_GREP_CPU` and `ENTIRE_GRAPH_GREP_THREADS` override the budget and `GOMAXPROCS` caps it, which is why the benchmark (at `GOMAXPROCS=2`) gets 2. **Under the machine budget**, graph-client asks for the engine's permits in one request at its spawn (4 here, the interactive cap), after Graph's in-process locks, and sets `ENTIRE_GRAPH_GREP_CPU` to the permits granted (with `ENTIRE_GRAPH_GREP_THREADS=1`), so the engine's pool is exactly the grant; a cut grant gives a smaller pool, never a larger one.
4. _More than one query at a time_ only once the bench shows the engine's prepared cache is safe to read concurrently. Each query would still take Graph's in-process locks first and its grant second.

**Commit preparation.** Its child is one Bun process plus `git`. Reduce first: confirm that a burst reuses trees and blobs (PC15's coalescing) and skip unchanged blobs when updating the index. Then split the chunking and parsing of changed blobs across a small pool of workers inside the child (Effect's `Pool`), sized by the child's one background grant. Bursts stay newest first.

## 14. When to stop

"As optimised as we understand we can be" (10.91) is reached when all four hold under the realistic background load:

1. No known way to reduce the work saves more than half of it at little cost.
2. Throughput at the chosen concurrency is within 10% of the fitted peak, so more parallelism would buy little.
3. The probe's 95th percentile stays within 1.5 times idle.
4. A Graph query's 95th percentile is within 2 times its idle time, against about 18 times today.

Then the sizes are recorded and the work stops. It reopens only when a ruling, a new kind of heavy work, or telemetry moves one of these.

**What to build first, and what waits.** The owner does not need this hill-climbed now, so the first local slice is the simplest design that is correct:

- _First:_ the scheduler (section 6) in the worker; the budget socket with the worker's claim and the version check; passing the grant's descriptor to the child (relying on Bun's internal, pinned by a test); `HeavyWork.start({ permits, class })` wired into the nine spawn sites of section 4, under the nesting rule, with the encoder's thread count set per request from its grant (or, as the fallback, a fixed exempt grant of 4); the floor; the class and nice rules; the model-call permit placed per route; and Graph's first application as 10.91 names it (section 13), already built and measured by the Graph lane: alias groups split into their alternatives, and the bounded pool of 4 scans of 1 thread, identical output. What the first slice adds is the grant: graph-client takes it at the engine's spawn and sets `ENTIRE_GRAPH_GREP_CPU` from it. The minimal Graph bench is done; the full bench of section 10 is later.
- _Later, when measured or needed:_ the full bench of section 10 and the sizes it chooses; deadlines of their own for Neuve's Bun children and for Graph preparation; re-declaring grants after a host crash (until then the new host may over-admit by the work still running, bounded by those children's deadlines); the brake (section 11), once the bench shows outside load mis-sizes the budget; sharing one budget with devcontainers.
- _Hosted only:_ leases in Durable Object storage, the outbox, a queue per class, Workflow steps and Containers.

## 16. Conflicts with existing rulings

- **10.91 (enforced through the job queue's slots).** Round 0's ledger file would have been a second admission point, polled, beside the queue. MB3 keeps one: the slots draw from the budget the worker hosts. Outside processes asking over a socket is still an addition to 10.91's wording, which the owner should confirm.
- **PC15 (10.75, commit bursts) and the M3 proposal's bounded-CPU row** say the background slot starts no new heavy child while a review runs. MB6 replaces that with a reserved share; the old rule becomes one setting of it.
- **XC16 (10.77, isolation and bounded CPU)** fixes "one Graph and one encoder", 2 `git` threads and nice 10 for every child. MB1, MB3 and MB15 replace the fixed per-process counts with grants; MB11 moves interactive children to nice 0.
- **10.15, 10.26 and 10.42 (a review never takes more than 10 minutes; that is its whole lifetime; new work stops at 9:45).** Round 1 left permit waits out of the lifetime; MB12 now counts them and withdraws waiting requests at the cut-off, so these rulings stand unchanged.
- **6.9 (no always-on service, and a worker that exits when idle).** The host is a worker that exists only while work does, and MB14 reads the load when asked rather than running a watcher; the probe of section 10 runs in the bench only. But a hosting worker may not exit idle while other repositories' or sessions' grants, claims or waiters are open (section 5's shared work), so one repository's worker can be kept alive by another's work. That refines the idle exit (D19) and needs the owner's agreement.
- **6.10 (never unbounded, on any machine) for sandboxed sessions.** Settled by 10.93: sandboxed sessions are allowed to reach the budget socket in their sandbox settings. A session without that setting runs at the floor, one thread at nice 19, and says so.
- **6.8 (the harness runtime is `effect/workflow`) on Cloudflare.** Running review runs as Cloudflare Workflows would replace `effect/workflow`'s engine there; keeping 6.8 means running its engine on Durable Object storage instead. Needs a ruling when the hosted work starts.
- **10.31 (two slots, one job each)** stays: MB3 adds a grant before a heavy child starts, not more jobs per slot.
- **10.90 (a fix that changes which matches Graph returns needs the owner's go).** MB15 is designed to change no match; the bench checks identical output first.
- **8.2 (no time lease anywhere) on hosted platforms.** Cloudflare Queues and Pub/Sub recover abandoned work by a timed limit (an invocation's wall limit, a visibility timeout, an acknowledgement deadline). The local adapter keeps 8.2; a hosted adapter cannot. 8.12 already foresaw a hosted queue such as BullMQ, which also uses timed locks, so this needs the owner to scope 8.2 to the local adapter. The Cloudflare adapter's budget grants are timed locks too: a grant is a leased row in the Durable Object that expires unless renewed, because its WebSockets cannot be trusted to report a dead holder.
- **8.6 (Records live in `.git/neuve/`, plus a user-level list of repositories).** The host claim adds a second user-level file, `budget.sqlite`, beside that list. It holds the host's claim only, no records, but it is new user-level state and needs the owner's agreement.
- **8.3 (Records is a library over one SQLite file, with no server) on Cloudflare.** A Durable Object per repository is a small server for that repository's records and queue. It is the cleanest mapping, but it is a different process model from 8.3's, and needs a ruling when the hosted work starts.
- **10.31 (two slots) on hosted platforms** becomes a queue per class rather than two slots inside one worker; the meaning (reviews never wait behind background work) is unchanged.
- **Vocabulary.** The owner chose `neuve capacity` for the command (10.93), avoiding the _query context budget_. It shows each job's threads: one unit of the budget is one thread, so "slot" keeps its meaning of the worker's two job positions (10.31), and "permit" and "grant" stay internal words of this design.

## 17. Open points

- Whether a worker started by an installed launchd agent inherits background class (the reviewer verified `taskpolicy`'s inheritance, not launchd's).
- The exact sandbox setting that lets sandboxed sessions reach `/tmp/neuve-<uid>/budget.sock` (10.93), checked in a real sandboxed session.
- Passing the grant's descriptor relies on Bun's internal `_handle.fd`; whether a documented way (Bun's "socket-fd" stdio option) can replace it.
- Whether Graph's `git` children inherit the grant's descriptor through Go's `os/exec`, as believed.
- Whether the Graph engine's prepared cache is safe for concurrent reads.
- Whether a devcontainer should share the host's budget by mounting the socket directory.
- Memory as a second dimension: every process loads its own encoder model. One encoder per machine may be needed; not proposed until measured.
- On Cloudflare: where Graph's clones and snapshot cache would live, since a Container's disk is lost when it sleeps; the lease length for grants; and whether `effect/workflow`'s engine (6.8) can run on Cloudflare Workflows or needs its own Durable Object engine.
- On the container path: whether named Cloud Tasks refuse repeat names long enough to help de-duplication, and Pub/Sub's exactly-once option, neither read here.
- A budget shared across a fleet of instances beyond the queue's dispatch limit is out of scope.
- The starting thresholds (1.5 times idle, 2 times idle, the brake's 1.0 and 0.7) are for the owner to confirm.
